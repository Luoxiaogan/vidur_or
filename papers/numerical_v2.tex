\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the WAIT and Nested WAIT algorithms on two classes of workloads: synthetic Poisson arrivals (Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}) and a real prompt distribution sampled from lmsys-chat-1m (Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}). On every workload we benchmark our algorithm against two widely deployed inference schedulers, vLLM~\citep{kwon2023efficient} and Sarathi~\citep{agrawal2023sarathi}; vLLM serves new arrivals first under FCFS, while Sarathi combines chunked prefill with a strict admission cap. We use Vidur~\citep{agrawal2024vidur} as the discrete-event simulator, configured to model a single NVIDIA A100~80\,GB serving Llama-2-7B with KV-cache block size $16$~tokens. Vidur's iteration-time predictions agree with direct vLLM measurements on a physical A100 to within $1.5\%$ MAPE on the batch-size range used in our experiments; we relegate this validation to Appendix~\ref{app:sim_fidelity}, together with the per-QPS tuned hyperparameters and complementary experiments.

The presentation throughout this section follows the standard queueing diagnostic. In an open system, throughput equals the arrival rate $\lambda$ in any rate region where the policy keeps the queue positive recurrent (i.e.\ stable), so throughput differences between policies reflect the size of their respective stability regions. We therefore plot mean end-to-end latency as a function of $\lambda$ and read each policy's transition point $\mu$, the largest $\lambda$ at which we observe bounded mean latency, off the divergence point of its curve. The same $\mu$ values appear as the kink positions of the corresponding effective-throughput curves.

\textbf{Hardware and iteration-time model.} The simulated A100~80\,GB pool holds $C = 29{,}952$ KV-cache blocks ($\approx 479{,}000$ tokens at $16$~tokens per block). Iteration time follows Equation~\eqref{eq:time_consump} with the coefficients $d_0 \approx 276$\,ms and $d_1 \approx 0.0115$\,ms per KV-cache token that we fit to direct vLLM measurements on a physical A100 (Appendix~\ref{app:sim_fidelity}, Figure~\ref{fig:sim_fidelity}).

\textbf{Common batch budget.} All three policies use a per-iteration batch budget $B = 512$ (requests). Sarathi additionally uses chunked prefill with chunk size $512$ tokens per iteration and an admission cap of $512$ requests; vLLM uses its default watermark fraction $0.01$ (reserving $1\%$ of $C$ as a safety margin); WAIT and Nested WAIT use chunked prefill with chunk size $256$ on the synthetic workloads (Appendix~\ref{app:sim_fidelity}).

\textbf{Threshold $\sum_k n_k$ (WAIT / Nested WAIT).} The total threshold is the single knob that the operator tunes at the workload level. It upper-bounds the in-system request count and thus caps the equilibrium memory at $M^{*} \leq (\sum_k n_k) \cdot M^*_{\max}$, well below $C$; WAIT and Nested WAIT therefore never trigger KV-cache eviction, whereas vLLM and Sarathi rely on watermarks and on-demand eviction to stay within the same capacity (Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}, Table~\ref{tab:eviction}). We choose $\sum_k n_k$ so that the fluid-optimality constraint of the corresponding theorem is satisfied at every tested arrival rate. For the single-type workload (prefill $512$, decode $20$), the constraint $\Delta T(n_{1:m}) \leq n_j / \lambda_j$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:wait_thresholds} gives $\sum_k n_k = 21$ (with $M^{*} \approx 14{,}400$ blocks, i.e.\ about $48\%$ of $C$). For the multi-type workload W3 (a $70/30$ mix of prefill-$512$ decode-$20$ and prefill-$512$ decode-$50$), the nested ratio constraint $n_{k+1}/n_k > p_k$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:nested_wait_thresholds} gives $\sum_k n_k = 22$ ($M^{*} \approx 17{,}500$ blocks). For the real lmsys workload, $\sum_k n_k$ is tuned per QPS on a small grid (Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}) and never exceeds $M^{*} \approx 12{,}000$ blocks. The per-segment thresholds $n_k$ are then derived from $\sum_k n_k$ as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}.


\subsection{Synthetic Arrivals}
\label{sec:exp_synthetic}

We consider two synthetic Poisson workloads. The single-type workload \texttt{p512d20} has prefill length $512$ and decode length $20$, isolating the effect of WAIT's threshold mechanism (Algorithm~\ref{alg:wait}). The multi-type workload W3 mixes $70\%$ \texttt{p512d20} with $30\%$ \texttt{p512d50}, exercising the segment design of Nested WAIT (Algorithm~\ref{alg:nested_wait}) on prompts that share the prefill profile but differ in decode length.

\paragraph{Single-type results (WAIT).}
Figure~\ref{fig:single_type_rate} shows mean end-to-end latency (left, stretched-symlog scale to magnify the stable region) and effective throughput (right) versus arrival rate $\lambda$. WAIT sustains bounded latency up to $\lambda \approx 23$, whereas Sarathi and vLLM lose stability near $\lambda \approx 21$ and $\lambda \approx 19$, respectively. WAIT achieves the smallest mean latency at every rate in the swept range $\lambda \in \{12, \dots, 24\}$, with the gap to Sarathi widening monotonically from ${\sim}2\%$ at $\lambda = 12$ to over $70\%$ at $\lambda = 23$. The right panel confirms the same ordering in throughput terms: WAIT remains on the ideal $\lambda$-line further than the two baselines.

\paragraph{Stability boundaries are not finite-horizon artifacts.}
The stability boundaries we read off Figures~\ref{fig:single_type_rate}--\ref{fig:multi_type_rate} are defined as a long-horizon property: the largest $\lambda$ at which the mean latency of an open queue stays bounded as the simulation horizon $N \to \infty$. For an unstable policy at a given $\lambda$, the empirical mean latency from a length-$N$ simulation grows roughly linearly in $N$ (because the queue length itself accumulates linearly in elapsed time), whereas a stable policy's empirical mean latency converges to a finite limit. We therefore verify that the divergence points in Figures~\ref{fig:single_type_rate}--\ref{fig:multi_type_rate} are not finite-$N$ noise by sweeping the horizon at the two arrival rates closest to Nested WAIT's transition. Figure~\ref{fig:fin_horizon} plots mean latency on W3 at $\lambda \in \{22, 23\}$ as $N$ grows from $2{,}000$ to $20{,}000$. At $\lambda = 22$ Nested WAIT's mean latency converges to a finite plateau, whereas Sarathi's mean latency grows roughly linearly in $N$ -- exactly the signature of an unstable open queue. At $\lambda = 23$ both policies are unstable, but Nested WAIT remains $30\%$--$50\%$ below Sarathi at every horizon. The fact that the gap between the two curves grows (rather than shrinks) with $N$ confirms that Figures~\ref{fig:single_type_rate}--\ref{fig:multi_type_rate} report actual stability boundaries, not artifacts of the chosen simulation budget.

\paragraph{Memory safeguard near capacity.}
The threshold $\sum_k n_k$ is more than a performance knob: it is the mechanism by which WAIT keeps the KV-cache footprint bounded by construction, and so prevents the eviction cascades that destabilize watermark-based admission policies in near-capacity regimes. We illustrate this with a long-decode workload (\texttt{p512d1000}) at $\lambda = 4.0$ with the KV-cache pool intentionally tightened to $60\%$ of the A100 block budget. Table~\ref{tab:eviction} contrasts the two mechanisms over a $10{,}000$-request run. Sarathi over-admits and triggers a runaway eviction cascade ($3{,}222$ evictions, mean latency $78.1$\,s); WAIT's explicit threshold caps the in-system request count and never evicts, attaining $31.3$\,s mean latency on the same workload. The eviction count is not a performance metric per se but the proximate cause of the latency gap: each eviction discards a partially decoded request whose remaining tokens must be redone after re-admission, multiplying the effective work per completion.

\subsection{Real Workload: lmsys-chat-1m}
\label{sec:exp_real}

The synthetic experiments above intentionally fix the prompt structure (one or two types, sharp prefill/decode lengths) so that each algorithm's stability boundary can be read cleanly off the latency curve. Real LLM-serving workloads, by contrast, contain heavy-tailed mixes of short and long prompts whose decode lengths are unknown at admission time: the regime that Nested WAIT (Algorithm~\ref{alg:nested_wait}) was designed to handle. The purpose of this section is to confirm, on a real prompt distribution, that the synthetic stability ordering carries over -- and to give intuition for how Nested WAIT's per-type thresholds adapt to a real workload.

\paragraph{Workload.}
We sample $50{,}000$ prompts from lmsys-chat-1m~\citep{zheng2023lmsys}, an open dataset of $\sim$1M human-LLM conversations. The sampled prompts have prefill lengths concentrated near $35$~tokens (median $30$, $95$th percentile $120$) with a heavy right tail, and decode lengths spanning $1$ to $500$~tokens (median $95$, $95$th percentile $380$); roughly $22\%$ of prompts have decode length $\leq 50$, and $10\%$ have decode length $> 300$. Arrivals are generated as a Poisson process at varying QPS, with the same simulator, hardware, and Sarathi / vLLM settings as in Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}.

\paragraph{Segment construction for Nested WAIT.}
Nested WAIT (Algorithm~\ref{alg:nested_wait}) operates on $m$ segments, each corresponding to a range of decode stages and each carrying its own admission threshold $n_k$. A prompt currently at decode stage $s$ belongs to segment $\lceil s / (L/m) \rceil$, where $L = 500$ is the maximum decode length covered by the data; it transitions from segment $k$ to segment $k{+}1$ once it decodes past stage $k \cdot L/m$. The threshold $n_k$ caps the number of prompts simultaneously in segment $k$. Because segments track the current decode stage rather than the total decode length, Nested WAIT does not require any prediction of how long a prompt will decode.

The segment count $m$ is the one structural knob specific to Nested WAIT on a real workload. Table~\ref{tab:segments_ablation} shows the effect of varying $m$ at fixed $\sum_k n_k$. Too few segments ($m = 10$) collapse decode stages with very different memory footprints into one threshold, and Nested WAIT cannot outperform Sarathi at any QPS. Too many segments ($m \geq 100$) force the $n_k$'s to be small integers -- with many segments at $n_k = 1$ -- which fragments the pipeline and reduces batch efficiency. The sweet spot sits near $m = 50$, the value we use throughout this section.

\paragraph{Threshold allocation across segments.}
The operator does not set the fifty thresholds $n_1, \dots, n_m$ individually. Instead, only the scalar total budget $\sum_k n_k$ is tuned at the workload level, and the per-segment thresholds are derived automatically to satisfy the fluid-optimality ratio constraint established for Nested WAIT (Equation~\eqref{eq:nested_wait_thresholds}):
\[
\frac{n_{k+1}}{n_k} > p_k, \qquad p_k = \frac{\sum_{j > k} \lambda_j}{\sum_{j \geq k} \lambda_j}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, m-1,
\]
where $p_k$ is the conditional probability that a prompt at segment $k$ continues past the segment boundary into segment $k{+}1$. The constraint says that thresholds may not shrink faster than the traffic itself as we move into longer-decode segments; if $n_{k+1}/n_k \leq p_k$ the late segments starve relative to the prompts that would eventually decode into them. We therefore set $n_k$ proportional to the traffic $f_k = \sum_{j \geq k} \lambda_j$ reaching segment $k$, with a small additive margin so that, after integer rounding against the budget $\sum_k n_k$, the ratio $n_{k+1}/n_k$ is strictly greater than $p_k$. Because $f_k$ is non-increasing in $k$ with $f_1 = 1$, the head segments receive the largest thresholds and the long-decode tail the smallest.

Concretely, on the lmsys workload with $\sum_k n_k = 30$ at QPS $= 60$, the allocation yields $(n_1, n_2, n_3, n_4, n_5) = (4, 3, 3, 2, 2)$ for the five head segments (decode stages $1$--$50$, reached by $100\%$ of prompts); $n_k \in \{1, 2\}$ for segments $k = 6, \dots, 15$ (stages $51$--$150$, reached by ${\sim}78\%$); $n_k \in \{0, 1\}$ for $k = 16, \dots, 30$ (stages $151$--$300$, reached by ${\sim}33\%$); and $n_k = 0$ for the tail $k = 31, \dots, 50$ (stages $301$--$500$, reached by ${<}10\%$). About $20$ of the $50$ segments carry a positive threshold at this budget; the remaining $30$ are dormant. Increasing $\sum_k n_k$ activates more tail segments and enlarges the head; decreasing it shrinks both. The operator therefore tunes a single scalar while the fluid-derived allocation takes care of the per-segment values -- consistent with the asymptotic-optimality analysis of Theorem~\ref{thm:nested_wait_heavy_traffic}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lmsys_rate} sweeps QPS from $10$ to $150$, with finer granularity around each policy's transition so that the divergence point falls on an observed data point. The stability ordering carries over directly from synthetic: vLLM at QPS $\approx 35$, Sarathi at $\approx 55$, Nested WAIT at $\approx 65$. Nested WAIT achieves the lowest mean latency at every QPS, with at least a $10\%$ improvement over Sarathi in the stable region and over $50\%$ near the boundary. The intuition is that real heavy-tailed prompts contain many short-decode requests that can complete quickly under tight admission, alongside a long-decode minority that would otherwise stall the queue; Nested WAIT's per-type thresholds let the short-decode types maintain throughput without being held up by the long-decode tail, an effect that single-policy schedulers like vLLM and Sarathi cannot replicate. The fact that the same stability ordering appears on synthetic and real workloads -- with comparable relative advantages -- supports the view that the advantage of Nested WAIT is structural (an explicit per-type threshold) rather than tied to a specific workload.
