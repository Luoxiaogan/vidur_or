\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the WAIT and Nested WAIT algorithms across a range of workloads, comparing them against two widely deployed inference schedulers: vLLM~\citep{kwon2023efficient}, which prioritizes new arrivals under a first-come-first-serve (FCFS) policy, and Sarathi~\citep{agrawal2023sarathi}, which combines chunked prefill with a strict admission cap. Our presentation is organized around the central diagnostic suggested by queueing theory: how mean end-to-end latency scales with the arrival rate, and where each policy's stability region ends. Section~\ref{sec:exp_setup} describes the simulator, hardware, workloads, and parameters. Section~\ref{sec:exp_stability} presents the main mean-latency-versus-rate comparison and reports the resulting stability orderings on synthetic and real workloads, together with a finite-horizon scaling check that confirms the divergence points are not numerical artifacts.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Simulator and hardware.}
We run all experiments on Vidur~\citep{agrawal2024vidur}, a discrete-event simulator for LLM inference engines that has been calibrated against profiling traces collected on real GPUs. The simulated platform is a single NVIDIA A100~80\,GB serving Llama-2-7B with tensor- and pipeline-parallelism degree one and a KV-cache block size of $16$~tokens. To verify that the simulator's iteration-time predictions match real GPU behavior in the regime relevant to our experiments, we additionally validate Vidur against direct vLLM measurements on a physical A100; the comparison is shown as Figure~\ref{fig:sim_fidelity} in Appendix~\ref{app:sim_fidelity} and confirms a mean absolute percentage error below $2\%$ on this range.

\paragraph{Algorithms compared.}
We compare three policies on every workload: vLLM, Sarathi, and the WAIT (resp.\ Nested WAIT) algorithm of Algorithm~\ref{alg:wait} (resp.\ Algorithm~\ref{alg:nested_wait}). Throughout this section, the single-type setting (with known output length) uses WAIT and the multi-type / unknown-output settings use Nested WAIT. Sarathi is configured with chunk size $512$ and an admission cap of $512$; vLLM uses a watermark of $0.01$ on the KV-cache pool. WAIT uses chunk size $256$ and a total threshold $\sum_k n_k = 21$; Nested WAIT uses a $(\mathrm{cs}, \sum_k n_k)$ that varies with the workload, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:exp_setup}. All three policies share the same simulator, hardware, and KV-cache settings.

\paragraph{Workloads.}
We consider three workloads, each with Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$ (queries per second):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-type} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_stability}, Figure~\ref{fig:single_type_rate}): prefill length $512$ and decode length $20$ (denoted \texttt{p512d20}); this is the canonical workload for the WAIT setting and isolates the effect of the threshold mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Multi-type W3} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_stability}, Figure~\ref{fig:multi_type_rate}): a $70/30$ mixture of \texttt{p512d20} and \texttt{p512d50}; the two types share the prefill profile but differ in decode length, exercising the segment design of Nested WAIT.
    \item \textbf{Real (lmsys-chat-1m)} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_stability}, Figure~\ref{fig:lmsys_rate}): $50{,}000$ prompts sampled from \citet{zheng2023lmsys}, with prefill lengths centered around $35$ tokens and decode lengths spanning $1$ to $500$ tokens; the prompts are bucketed into $50$ equal-width decode-length bins for Nested WAIT's segment construction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Memory safeguard.}
The threshold $\sum_k n_k$ used by WAIT and Nested WAIT is an explicit upper bound on the number of in-system requests, and therefore caps the KV-cache footprint at $(\sum_k n_k) \cdot M^*_{\max}$ where $M^*_{\max}$ is the largest per-request memory profile across active types. Because this bound is enforced by construction at every batching decision, no eviction is required to satisfy the memory constraint $C \geq M^*$ assumed in Sections~\ref{sec:wait}--\ref{sec:nested_wait}. By contrast, vLLM and Sarathi rely on watermarks and on-demand eviction to satisfy the same constraint, which has consequences for stability that we examine next.

\paragraph{Parameters.}
Table~\ref{tab:exp_setup} summarizes the key parameters: the per-iteration batch budget $B$, the equilibrium memory $M^*$, the capacity $C$ (in KV-cache blocks), and the threshold $\sum_k n_k$ used by WAIT and Nested WAIT. The iteration-time intercept and slope $(d_0, d_1)$ from Vidur's profile of A100 + Llama-2-7B are $d_0 \approx 276$\,ms and $d_1 \approx 0.0115$\,ms per KV-cache token (cf.\ Figure~\ref{fig:sim_fidelity}).

\subsection{Stability Region: Mean Latency versus Arrival Rate}
\label{sec:exp_stability}

In an open queueing system, throughput equals the arrival rate $\lambda$ in any rate region where the policy keeps the queue positive recurrent (i.e.\ stable); throughput differences between policies therefore reflect the size of their respective stability regions. The standard diagnostic is to plot mean end-to-end latency as a function of $\lambda$ and read the divergence point off each curve. We adopt this diagnostic throughout the section. The vertical dashed lines and labelled arrows in each panel mark each policy's transition point $\mu$, the largest $\lambda$ at which we observe bounded mean latency; the same $\mu$ values appear as the kink positions of the throughput panel.

\paragraph{Single-type (WAIT).}
Figure~\ref{fig:single_type_rate} reports the result for the canonical single-type workload. The left panel shows mean end-to-end latency on a stretched-symlog scale that magnifies the stable region; the right panel shows the corresponding effective throughput, defined as the completion rate that each policy is able to sustain. As $\lambda$ increases, every policy eventually leaves its stability region, and the mean-latency curve diverges. WAIT sustains bounded latency up to $\lambda \approx 23$, whereas Sarathi loses stability near $\lambda \approx 21$ and vLLM near $\lambda \approx 19$. At every rate point in the swept range $\lambda \in \{12, 13, \dots, 24\}$, WAIT attains the smallest mean latency among the three policies; the gap relative to Sarathi widens monotonically from approximately $2\%$ at $\lambda = 12$ to over $70\%$ at $\lambda = 23$. The right panel reports the same ordering in throughput terms: WAIT remains on the ideal $\lambda$-line further than the two baselines and saturates at the highest sustainable throughput. The fact that the divergence points are well-separated, even though all three policies share the same hardware and memory budget, demonstrates that the threshold-based admission rule of WAIT enlarges the stability region beyond what the admission heuristics of vLLM and Sarathi achieve.

\paragraph{Multi-type W3 (Nested WAIT).}
Figure~\ref{fig:multi_type_rate} reports the analogue for the multi-type W3 workload, where Nested WAIT must allocate a per-segment threshold across the two prompt types. The qualitative picture is the same as in the single-type case: vLLM diverges first (near $\lambda \approx 18$), Sarathi second (near $\lambda \approx 20$), and Nested WAIT last (near $\lambda \approx 22$). At every rate in $\lambda \in \{12, \dots, 24\}$, Nested WAIT achieves the lowest mean latency; the relative gap to Sarathi grows from approximately $6\%$ at low load to over $40\%$ near the boundary, and Nested WAIT remains the best choice well into the regime where Sarathi has become unstable.

\paragraph{Real workload: lmsys-chat-1m.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lmsys_rate} reports the same comparison on the lmsys-chat-1m workload, swept over QPS $\in \{10, 15, \dots, 70, 80, 90, \dots, 150\}$ with finer granularity around each policy's transition. The transition ordering is preserved: vLLM at QPS $\approx 35$, Sarathi at $\approx 55$, Nested WAIT at $\approx 65$. Every QPS shows Nested WAIT improving over Sarathi by at least $10\%$ in mean latency, with the gap exceeding $50\%$ at the boundary (QPS $\in \{60, 65\}$). On a real, heavy-tailed prompt distribution Nested WAIT therefore retains the same qualitative advantages it displays on the synthetic workloads.

\paragraph{Finite-horizon scaling check.}
Mean latency from a simulation of $N$ requests is a finite-horizon estimate of the long-run average; for an unstable policy the estimate grows without bound as $N \to \infty$, while for a stable policy it converges to a finite limit. To verify that the divergence points reported above are not finite-horizon artifacts, Figure~\ref{fig:fin_horizon} shows mean end-to-end latency at the two arrival rates closest to Nested WAIT's transition (multi-type W3, $\lambda \in \{22, 23\}$) as the simulation horizon grows from $N = 2{,}000$ to $N = 20{,}000$. At $\lambda = 22$, Sarathi's mean latency grows by a factor of $5.6$ over this horizon range -- the empirical signature of an unstable open queue -- while Nested WAIT's mean latency grows by only a factor of $1.6$ and is visibly approaching a finite plateau. At $\lambda = 23$, both policies are in their unstable regime and the curves grow steeply, but Nested WAIT remains $30\%$--$50\%$ below Sarathi at every horizon. The stability ordering established by Figures~\ref{fig:single_type_rate}--\ref{fig:lmsys_rate} therefore reflects an actual property of the policies, not a reading of the simulation budget.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Experiments_pdf/figure_E_stability.pdf}
