\section{Additional Experiments}
\label{app:additional_experiments}

This appendix collects experiments that address specific reviewer comments without crowding the main text. Section~\ref{app:no_wait} compares WAIT with a threshold-only variant that skips the accumulation wait, the alternative algorithm suggested by Reviewer~2. Section~\ref{app:gpu_validation} reports the real-GPU validation in three parts: a simulator-vs-GPU iteration-time comparison, a single-type GPU sweep that mirrors Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}, and a real-data GPU sweep that mirrors Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}. Section~\ref{app:multi_seed} reports multi-seed reproducibility. Section~\ref{app:pd_disagg} extends the evaluation to a prefill-decode-disaggregated deployment.

\subsection{Threshold without waiting}
\label{app:no_wait}

Reviewer~2 asks: ``what happens if the algorithm does not wait -- i.e., still uses the threshold $n_j$ but processes decoding requests without waiting for $n_j$ prompts to accumulate?'' We refer to this variant as \emph{WAIT-no-wait}: the per-segment thresholds $n_k$ are still enforced (so the KV cache footprint remains bounded as in the main WAIT algorithm), but a segment is scheduled in each iteration regardless of whether its entry-stage inventory has reached $n_k$. The accumulation requirement is the only thing removed.

Table~\ref{tab:no_wait} reports mean end-to-end latency for six Vidur workload scenarios that span single-type, multi-type, and memory-limited regimes. The two variants are very close on easy regimes (the memory-limited scenario is a tie), and in several scenarios the no-wait variant is actually lower-latency, because skipping the accumulation wait removes small scheduling delays that are unnecessary once the threshold is satisfied on average. The real-GPU experiments on SGLang (Section~\ref{app:gpu_validation}, Table~\ref{tab:sglang_realtrace}) corroborate this picture but also identify a regime in which the accumulation wait pays off: on the lmsys-chat-1m sweep, the per-rate tuned WAIT configuration attains its largest improvement of $+9.2\%$ at $\lambda = 0.4$, where the accumulation wait is the active mechanism. The choice between the two variants is therefore regime-dependent. The threshold itself is what provides the asymptotic stability guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:wait}; the accumulation wait acts as a latency refinement that helps in specific operating regimes.

\subsection{Real-GPU validation}
\label{app:gpu_validation}

We complement the simulator-based experiments of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} with end-to-end runs on a physical GPU. The setup is a single NVIDIA A100~80\,GB serving Llama-2-7B through the SGLang~0.5.7 production serving engine; WAIT is implemented as a native SGLang scheduler module, so the comparison is independent of Vidur. We report three pieces of GPU evidence: a check that the simulator's iteration-time predictions agree with direct GPU measurements; an end-to-end comparison on a single-type workload that mirrors the synthetic experiment of Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}; and an end-to-end comparison on a real-data workload that mirrors the lmsys-chat-1m experiment of Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}.

\paragraph{Simulator vs GPU.}
To verify that the simulator's iteration-time model matches real GPU behavior in the regime relevant to Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, we compare Vidur's predictions against direct measurements on the same A100~80\,GB GPU running Llama-2-7B served by vLLM. We evaluate $25$ batch-size configurations $B \in \{1, 2, 4, \ldots, 128\}$ at fixed prefill length $256$ and decode length $20$, which together span the batch-size range used in our experiments. As Figure~\ref{fig:sim_fidelity} shows, Vidur's predictions track the GPU measurements to within mean absolute percentage error $1.51\%$ ($R^2 = 0.9957$). The fitted iteration-time intercept and slope are $d_0 \approx 276$\,ms and $d_1 \approx 0.0115$\,ms per KV cache token, in line with the linear time model of Equation~\eqref{eq:time_consump}. This justifies using the simulator as the basis for the throughput and stability comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Experiments_pdf/figure_A_sim_fidelity.pdf}
    \caption{Validation of the Vidur simulator against real NVIDIA A100~80\,GB GPU measurements. Each point is one batch-size configuration $B \in \{1, 2, 4, \ldots, 128\}$ ($N = 25$), with prefill $256$ and decode $20$, serving Llama-2-7B via vLLM. The dashed line is $y = x$. The linear iteration-time model of Equation~\eqref{eq:time_consump} achieves $R^2 = 0.9957$ and mean absolute percentage error $1.51\%$.}
    \label{fig:sim_fidelity}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Single-type workload on GPU.}
This sweep mirrors the synthetic experiment of Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic}: random prompts with input length $512$ and output length $20$, with Poisson arrivals at $\lambda \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 15\}$ requests/s and $500$ prompts per rate. The SGLang baseline uses \texttt{chunked\_prefill\_size}$=256$ and \texttt{prefill\_max\_requests}$=8$ (its default configuration triggers OOM at several mid-to-high rates and so cannot serve as a fair baseline). WAIT uses $\mathrm{tl} = 21$, applied uniformly across all rates, with an underload bypass that defers to SGLang's native scheduler when the request queue is empty. Figure~\ref{fig:sglang} shows mean end-to-end latency versus $\lambda$. WAIT improves over the baseline at every rate, with the gap widening from $\sim 6\%$ at $\lambda = 1$ to over $50\%$ at $\lambda = 15$ (average improvement $29.7\%$ across the sweep). The stability and latency ordering from Section~\ref{sec:exp_synthetic} carries over to the GPU.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Experiments_pdf/figure_H_sglang.pdf}
    \caption{Single-type GPU sweep on SGLang~0.5.7 (NVIDIA A100~80\,GB, Llama-2-7B, random prompts with input length $512$ and output length $20$): mean end-to-end latency versus arrival rate. WAIT improves over the (weakened) SGLang baseline at every rate tested, with a mean improvement of $29.7\%$ across the sweep.}

\paragraph{Real-data workload on GPU.}
This sweep mirrors the lmsys-chat-1m experiment of Section~\ref{sec:exp_real}: prompts sampled from the same dataset (heavy-tailed prefill lengths concentrated near $30$ tokens; decode lengths spanning $1$ to $500$ tokens with median $95$ and $95$th percentile $380$), with Poisson arrivals at $\lambda \in \{0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 0.7\}$ requests/s and $200$ prompts per rate. The SGLang server is restarted between rates so that each WAIT run is paired against a fresh baseline at the same rate. WAIT uses the total in-flight limit $\mathrm{tl}$ tuned per rate; the per-rate value of $\mathrm{tl}$ is given in Table~\ref{tab:sglang_realtrace}. Table~\ref{tab:sglang_realtrace} reports the per-rate paired-baseline improvement: WAIT improves over the SGLang baseline at every swept rate, with peak improvement of $+9.2\%$ at $\lambda = 0.4$ and an average of $+4.7\%$ across the seven rates. \subsection{Multi-seed reproducibility}
\label{app:multi_seed}

The Figures in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} report a single seed per configuration; the stability boundaries are insensitive to seed selection because they are defined as the divergence point of a long-horizon mean, but finite-$N$ variance could in principle move individual data points. Table~\ref{tab:multi_seed} reports the result of re-running the near-boundary arrival rates $\lambda \in \{22, 23\}$ on the single-type and multi-type workloads over five independent seeds.

WAIT's sample standard deviation is consistently an order of magnitude smaller than Sarathi's and vLLM's, because WAIT is deep in its stable region at these rates while the baselines are past theirs; the smaller variance reflects bounded-queue behavior. The range of five seeds preserves the stability ordering at every $(\text{workload}, \lambda)$ combination.

\subsection{Prefill-decode-disaggregated deployment}
\label{app:pd_disagg}

Production LLM serving increasingly uses a \emph{prefill-decode disaggregation} (PD) architecture: prefill is computed by one service and decode by another, with the KV cache shipped between them. The decode-side scheduler sees a stream of prompts whose prefill has already completed and needs to allocate decode-stage compute. This setting is a clean fit for our algorithm: every admitted prompt proceeds immediately to decode, so WAIT's threshold directly bounds the number of prompts that the decode-side service keeps in memory.

We implement this as a \texttt{p630d20} workload (post-prefill context length $630$ tokens, decode length $20$) on the decode side of a PD setup, with Poisson arrivals sweeping $\lambda \in \{100, 150, \ldots, 500\}$ requests/s. The baseline is vLLM's own PD scheduler (\texttt{vLLM (PD)}). Figure~\ref{fig:pd_disagg} shows mean end-to-end decode latency: WAIT improves over vLLM at every rate, with the relative gap ranging from $45\%$ at $\lambda = 100$ to $55\%$ at $\lambda = 500$. The algorithm's advantage therefore transfers directly to the PD-disaggregated architecture without modification.
