## Context You Should Keep In Mind

The paper is a major revision for OPRE-2025-04-1885, titled:

> Optimizing LLM Inference: Fluid-Guided Online Scheduling with Memory Constraints

The intended revision narrative is:

1. LLM inference is an open stochastic serving system with staged prefill/decode service and dynamic KV-cache memory growth.
2. The main operational problem is not unconstrained throughput maximization at a fixed arrival rate. In a stable open system, completed work is capped by offered load. The meaningful questions are stability-region expansion, effective throughput net of eviction, latency, and TTFT.
3. The distinct challenge is that memory consumption grows during decode; poor scheduling can trigger eviction-induced restarts even when capacity is theoretically sufficient for the ideal-fluid equilibrium.
4. The fluid model identifies an ideal-fluid equilibrium and a memory requirement \(M^*\) needed to support that equilibrium. \(M^*\) should not be framed as "throughput-optimal memory."
5. WAIT and Nested WAIT are threshold policies. Their main conceptual role is dimensionality reduction: they turn a high-dimensional memory-coupled scheduling problem into analyzable threshold/boundary queues.
6. Nested WAIT handles unknown output lengths through on-the-fly classification at decode segment boundaries; it should not sound like it requires output-length prediction.
7. The response to Reviewer 3 must be especially strong: avoid classical heavy-traffic claims, explain why the theory is nontrivial, and make clear why latency experiments over arrival-rate sweeps are meaningful.
8. Numerical results and response-letter claims must be internally consistent: simulator calibration, SGLang/A100 validation, long-decode eviction diagnostics, real-data tuning language, and "effective completion rate" vs token-level "effective throughput" must not conflict.

## Standards For The Audit

- Be adversarial but not speculative. Every criticism should cite a location or quote.
- Do not ask the authors to run new experiments unless the current claim cannot be defended without them.
- Separate "paper problem" from "response letter problem."
- Separate "actual inconsistency" from "could be clearer."
- Do not reward generic explanations. If wording is weak, propose stronger wording.
- Do not simply say "add more details." Say exactly what details are missing and where they should go.
- Prioritize issues that could cause rejection or reviewer distrust.
- If the response letter claims a change that the paper does not actually contain, mark it Critical.
- If the paper contains a fix but the response letter fails to highlight it, mark it Major or Minor depending on importance.
- If a reviewer concern is fully resolved, say so explicitly.
